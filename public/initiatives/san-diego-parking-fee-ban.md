# Information

San Diego City Council is considering an ordinance that would permanently ban the City from ever charging for parking at Mission Bay and other city parks and beaches.

While free parking sounds appealing, permanently giving up this option creates real problems:

- **Parking congestion**: Free, unlimited parking in Mission Bay already leads to long-term storage of vehicles and equipment, abandoned cars, and commercial businesses occupying public spaces meant for visitors. During busy periods, cars back up along West Mission Bay Drive all the way to Ingraham Street.
- **Paving over parks**: Mission Bay has already devoted up to 60% of parkland in some areas to parking. Building more parking to solve congestion would come at the expense of the parks themselves.
- **Deferred maintenance**: Balboa Park alone faces an estimated $1 billion in deferred maintenance, and Mission Bay recently almost had to close half of its restrooms. A modest parking fee could be a dedicated funding source for upkeep.
- **Climate goals**: Free, unlimited parking subsidizes driving and works against San Diego's climate strategy of reducing single-occupancy vehicle trips.

This ordinance would not eliminate an existing fee — it would permanently remove the City's ability to ever use one, even in the future when conditions change. A better approach is to preserve that flexibility, not lock it away.

**Tell the City Council to preserve the option of a modest parking fee, rather than banning it forever.**

# Content

Dear Councilmembers,

We believe this ordinance is a mistake because it would prevent the City from using a small parking fee in the future as a tool to manage parking and support our parks. Rather than permanently prohibiting this option, the City should retain the flexibility to respond to changing conditions and community needs.

Free Parking Creates Real Management Problems

Anyone who has tried to park in Mission Bay or San Diego's other popular scenic areas has seen the problems created by free, unlimited parking. Spaces are frequently used for long-term parking rather than short visits. Abandoned and broken-down vehicles can occupy spaces for extended periods. Commercial businesses, ranging from small kayak rental operations to large companies like Amazon, use public parking spaces to store equipment and vehicles. Large groups visiting for picnics, sporting events, and other activities often arrive in many single-occupancy vehicles. When parking is completely free and unlimited, there is little incentive to carpool, use transit, or otherwise use our limited parking supply efficiently.

These individual uses may seem insignificant, but collectively they create a serious problem. During busy periods, parking congestion in Mission Bay can become severe, with vehicles backing up along West Mission Bay Drive all the way to Ingraham Street. Simply building more parking is not a sustainable solution. Mission Bay has already devoted an enormous amount of parkland, up to 60% in some places, to parking. Adding still more paved parking would come at the expense of the very parks and recreational spaces that people are coming to enjoy.

A Modest Fee Can Improve Parking Availability

Instead, the City should have the ability to manage its existing parking supply. A modest parking fee could encourage more efficient use of spaces, discourage long-term storage of vehicles and equipment, and improve turnover so that visitors have a better chance of finding a space. We are not advocating for expensive parking fees that would make visiting our beaches inaccessible. We are asking the City to retain the option of using a small fee if and when parking management becomes necessary.

Parking Revenue Can Help Maintain Our Parks

Parking fees could also provide a modest but potentially important source of funding for park maintenance. Our parks around San Diego are increasingly facing existential maintenance debts. Mission Bay itself recently almost had to close half of its restrooms, and funding for restrooms in 2027 has not yet been secured. Balboa Park alone is facing an estimated $1 billion in deferred maintenance and infrastructure needs. When our parks are struggling to maintain basic facilities and facing enormous long-term costs, it is worth asking whether visitors who choose to drive and use scarce public parking should contribute something toward maintaining those public assets. A small parking fee would not solve these challenges on its own, but it could provide a stable, dedicated source of revenue while also helping the City manage demand for limited parking.

Free Beach Access Does Not Require Free Parking

There is also an important distinction between making parking free and making access to our beaches free. These are not the same thing. A person arriving by car is using substantially more public space and infrastructure than someone arriving by bicycle, on foot, or by transit. A modest parking fee would allow the City to recover at least some of the costs associated with providing and maintaining automobile access without charging people simply to enjoy our beaches and parks.

Equity Means Supporting More Than Driving

This is also an issue of equity. Many San Diegans cannot afford the substantial cost of owning, maintaining, insuring, and fueling a vehicle. If we permanently prioritize free parking over other forms of access, we risk directing public resources toward those who can afford to drive while doing less to improve access for everyone else. San Diego should be working toward beaches and parks that are accessible by transit, bicycle, walking, and automobile, not building a system in which free automobile parking is prioritized above all else, including maintaining the public space itself.

Free Parking Conflicts With the City's Climate Goals

Finally, permanently locking in free parking conflicts with the City's broader climate and transportation goals. San Diego's climate strategy calls for reducing reliance on single-occupancy vehicles and increasing the use of transit and active transportation. Providing unlimited free parking sends the opposite economic signal: it subsidizes driving and gives people little reason to consider other ways of reaching our parks and beaches. A modest parking fee is one of many tools the City could use to better align transportation policy with its stated climate goals.

We urge the City Council not to permanently eliminate this policy option. The City should retain the ability to use modest, carefully designed parking fees when they are appropriate to manage demand, improve access, and help maintain the parks that make San Diego such a desirable place to live and visit.

Free parking may sound appealing today, but permanently giving up the ability to manage parking in the future is not good public policy. Please preserve the City's flexibility to respond to congestion, maintenance needs, climate goals, and the changing ways San Diegans and visitors use our public spaces.
